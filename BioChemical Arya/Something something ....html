Glycolysis

Lactose is metabolized to lactic acid which can be catabolized (broken down into smaller molecules ) to form acetic and propionic acids, carbon dioxides, esters and alcohols by enzymes of \(\mu\)organisms. Glycolysis is defined as the metabolic process that breaks down glucose to produce energy, which is closely related to the activation, proliferation, differentiation, survival, and function of CD4 + T cells. Glycolysis occurs in both aerobic and anaerobic states. In aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters the citric acid cycle and undergoes oxidative phosphorylation leading to the net production of 32 ATP molecules. In anaerobic conditions, pyruvate converts to lactate through anaerobic glycolysis. Anaerobic respiration results in the production of 2 ATP molecules.5(https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK482303/#) Glucose is a hexose sugar, meaning it is a monosaccharide with six carbon atoms and six oxygen atoms. The first carbon has an attached aldehyde group, and the other five carbons have one hydroxyl group each. During glycolysis, glucose ultimately breaks down into pyruvate and energy; a total of 2 ATP is derived in the process (Glucose + 2 NAD+ + 2 ADP + 2 Pi –> 2 Pyruvate + 2 NADH + 2 H+ + 2 ATP + 2 H2O). The hydroxyl groups allow for phosphorylation. The specific form of glucose used in glycolysis is glucose 6-phosphate.

Lipolysis

The fat in the milk is broken down to form free fatty acids that may be then catabolized to form ketones, lactone and esters by rennet enzymes those to create the flavor in some variety of cheese. Lipolysis is defined as the hydrolytic cleavage of ester bonds in triglycerides (TGs), resulting in the generation of fatty acids (FAs) and glycerol. The two major TG pools in the body of vertebrates comprise intracellular TGs and plasma/nutritional TGs. Accordingly, this leads to the discrimination between intracellular and intravascular/gastrointestinal lipolysis, respectively. https://www.sciencedirect.com/referencework/9780123864574/pathobiology-of-human-disease

Proteolysis

Proteins are gradually broken down to form peptides and amino acids by the enzymes of the coagulant, the natural milk enzymes and the enzymes of the starter bacteria and other added microorganisms. The addition of species results in a high level of proteolysis in these cheese. The activities of the coagulant enzyme, the amount of the enzyme remaining in the curd and the amount of proteolysis in the curd and the amount of proteolysis are dependent on the amount of acid produced in the initial; stages of cheese making. The pH also controls the levels of moisture which in turn effects proteolysis. The final pH and the rate of pH decline determine the dissolution of colloidal calccium phosphate.
Proteolysis is the process in which the enzyme molecule (i.e the substrate) is degraded by a protease. The sites of proteolytic attack are determined mainly by the specificity of the protease, but also involves flexible parts of the substrate which are located at the surface. Factors which promote proteolytic activity are the temperature and partially denaturing conditions for the substrate. The effect of other conditions such as the pH and the presence of metal ions are also depending on the type of protease. https://www.sciencedirect.com/bookseries/progress-in-biotechnology

Wine Making

  • Wine making starts with crushing of grapes.
  • Crushed grape juice is bottled (after filtration) & allowed to ferment with the yeast naturally present in grape skin.
  • Different regions of grapes may have its own unique aroma & taste.
  • The principle monoterpenes found in wine are glucosides of:
    1. Linalool
    2. Nerol
    3. Geraniol
    4. \(\alpha\)-terpineol
  • \(\beta\)-glucosidase hydrolyzes the bond between glucose and the substrate on the anomeric carbon.
  • Natural hydrolysis of glycosidically bound terpenes in the acidic conditions of wine takes years, because \(\beta\)-glucosidase in wine acts at conditions far from optimum.
  • Grape-derived enzymes are often inactive under wine-making conditions:
    1. Low pH
    2. Presence of ethanol
    3. Phenolic compounds
    4. Sulphite salts
  • Fungal enzymes like pectinase are employed for fermentation. Also, glycosidase and glucanase are used.

To accelerate aroma development and improve product quality, exogenous microbial enzymes are introduced. Fungal preparations (from Aspergillus and Trichoderma species) provide: - Pectinases – degrade pectic polysaccharides, enhancing juice extraction and clarification.
- β-glucosidases – liberate terpenes by cleaving glycosidic linkages. - Glucanases – hydrolyze glucans that otherwise cause filtration problems.

The design of these enzyme cocktails requires process optimization to balance activity under acidic, ethanol-rich environments. In biochemical engineering terms, the challenge is to shift the enzyme activity profile closer to the actual operating conditions of fermentation.

  • Kinetics: Enzyme-catalyzed hydrolysis competes with chemical stability of glycosides; Michaelis–Menten kinetics can model release rates.
  • Process Control: Temperature, pH buffering, and oxygen transfer are tightly regulated to prevent loss of volatile compounds.
  • Flavor Engineering: Controlled enzyme addition enables targeted release of specific monoterpenes, leading to regional wine “terroir” profiles.
  • Downstream Processing: Enzyme-assisted clarification reduces filtration load and improves stability of the final product.

Vinegar Production

  • Conversion of alcohol in wine to acetic acid by Acetobacter spp.
  • Other compounds in different types of vinegar:
    1. Malic, citric, succinic, lactic acids
    2. Unfermented & unfermentable sugars
    3. Oxidized alcohols
  1. Alcoholic Fermentation (Anaerobic)
    • Carried out by yeast at 30–32 °C.
    • By-products: glycerol, succinic acid, amyl alcohol.
  2. Acetogenic Fermentation (Aerobic)
    • Carried out by Acetobacter aceti at 27–30 °C.
    • Ethanol → Acetic acid (oxidation).
  • Microorganisms Involved
    1. Acetobacter spp.
      • Acetobacter xylinum
      • Acetobacter europaeus
      • Acetobacter vinelandii
    2. Gluconobacter spp.
      • Gluconobacter oxydans

Industrial Methods

  1. Slow Vinegar Method (Orleans Method)
    • Wine is left in open vats.
    • Air + bacteria from atmosphere convert ethanol -> acetic acid.
    • A small amount of vinegar is added to fresh wine (as inoculum).
  2. Trickling Generator Method
    • Alcoholic liquid is resprinkled at the top of the tower.
    • Air enters through the middle, warms up, rises, and vents above.
    • Beechwood shavings or similar materials provide a surface for AAB (acetic acid bacteria) growth. Key features:
    • Tower has alternating solid packings (support for bacteria) and liquid layers.
    • Cooling water via intermediate heat exchanger controls temperature.
    • Sensors placed at different tower levels monitor temperature (29–35\(\degree\)C).
  3. Submerged Fermentation
    • A stirred medium containing alcohol is inoculated with Acetobacter or Gluconobacter spp.
    • Temperature range: 24–29 \(\degree\)C.
    • Bacteria grow in suspension with fine air bubbles supplied by an aerator.
    • Ethanol is oxidized to acetic acid. Key points:
    • Aeration must be vigorous (oxygen is limiting under acidic conditions).
    • Poor aeration → bacterial death.
    • Efficient cooling is necessary (fermentation is exothermic).
    • Submerged vinegar often turns turbid (high bacterial content), so filtration is required.

TriCarboxylic Acid Cycle

https://onlinesciencenotes.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/Krebs-cycle.png

The Krebs cycle is a series of reactions catalyzed by seven enzymes in mitochondria. Its function is to catalyze removal of electrons from nutrients and to transfer them to NAD+ and FAD, producing NADH plus H\(^+\), and FADH\(_2\), respectively. These reduced cofactors exist only momentarily in their reduced (or oxidized) forms as they continually accept and then donate electrons to the respiratory chain. The respiratory chain, composed of a number of cytochromes, uses electrons for reduction of O2 to water. This reduction process is accompanied by or coupled with the regeneration of ATP, that is, conversion of ADP back to ATP. The overall effect may be summarized thus: The Krebs cycle and respiratory chain are used for oxidizing nutrients to CO2 and for the production of energy.

The Krebs cycle, which is also called the citric acid cycle or the tricarboxylic acid cycle, was discovered by Hans Krebs in 1937, at Cambridge University in Great Britain (Holmes, 1993). https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780121348366/nutritional-biochemistry

In the presence of oxygen, a molecule of CO2 and two hydrogen atoms are split from pyruvic acid, and acetyl-coenzyme A (acetyl-CoA) is formed. Acetyl-CoA combines with oxaloacetic acid in the Krebs cycle to form the six-carbon atom citric acid. Citric acid is successively converted to five- and four-carbon acids as carbon dioxide molecules and hydrogen atoms are split off, and the cycle finally returns to oxaloacetic acid.  Becker and Deamer (1991) summarized the Krebs cycle as follows: Acetyl-CoA + 3H2O + 3NAD+ + FAD + ADP + Pi → 2CO2 + 3NADH + 3H\(^+\) + FADH2 + CoA-SH + ATP + H2O.

When the above equation is adjusted for the two cycles needed to metabolize both of the acetyl-CoA molecules derived from one molecule of glucose, the overall expression of glycolysis through pyruvate, and oxidative decarboxylation of pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, the summary equation from glucose for the Krebs cycle becomes (Becker and Deamer, 1991) Glucose + 6H2O + 10NAD+ + 2FAD + 4ADP + 4Pi → 6CO2 + 10NADH + 10H+ + 2FADH2 + 4ATP + 4H2O

The total energy yield from one molecule of glucose (including the yields of glycolysis, pyruvate to acetyl-CoA conversion, and the Krebs cycle) is 36 ATP (see p. 96 of Raven et al., 1992). https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780124241626/physiology-of-woody-plants
